01

The proposed theory

Psychological and social development across generations

Biological evolution is often described through simplified images of earlier primates gradually becoming modern humans. Human development, however, is not only biological. The environments in which people live, the institutions around them, the pressures they experience and the ideas available to them also change over time.

The central proposition of this theory is that families and social groups may develop enduring priorities, ambitions, fears, values and unresolved conflicts. These influences may be transmitted—deliberately or unintentionally—to later generations through upbringing, family stories, expectations, material circumstances, education, social networks and repeated patterns of behaviour.

Each person remains an individual. Their psychology is shaped by a unique combination of temperament, environment, treatment by others, opportunities, hardships, lessons and personal interpretation. The theory does not claim that a family has one fixed or conscious objective. Instead, it asks whether recurring family-level influences can produce patterns that resemble a continuing intergenerational “goal”.

These inherited or learned influences exist within a particular historical period. Victorian Britain, the Second World War and contemporary society contain different laws, class structures, technologies and social expectations. Values that were once dominant can weaken, be rejected or be reinterpreted. New priorities—including equality, diversity and inclusion—can alter the choices available to individuals and the standards by which behaviour is judged.

Under this framework, social evolution occurs partly because people inherit influences from the past while adapting them to current conditions. A pattern continues only where people remain connected to it, consciously or unconsciously. It can also break down when families separate, reject earlier values, lose contact, experience major disruption or deliberately choose a different direction.

02

Core propositions

Claims to be defined and tested

01

Individual formation

Each person develops through a distinct interaction of temperament, relationships, environment, education, opportunity, adversity and personal choice.

02

Intergenerational transmission

Families may pass down values, expectations, coping strategies, ambitions, fears, narratives and behavioural patterns without those influences being formally recorded.

03

Historical adaptation

Inherited influences are altered by the laws, technologies, economic conditions and social norms of the generation in which a person lives.

04

Alignment and conflict

People may support, resist or redirect the priorities around them. Shared aims may strengthen group action, while incompatible aims may produce division or estrangement.

05

Continuity is conditional

A family pattern is not permanent. It can weaken or end through separation, death, changing circumstances, conscious rejection or the formation of new relationships.

06

Societal consequences

Large numbers of interacting personal and family trajectories may influence institutions, culture, collective priorities and the direction of social change.

03

Investigation framework

Questions for further research

Definition

Can the proposed “goal” be defined in observable terms without implying that an entire family shares a single conscious intention?

Transmission

Which mechanisms—such as parenting, storytelling, economic position, trauma, education or social networks—best explain continuity between generations?

Variation

Why do siblings and relatives exposed to similar family influences often develop different beliefs, personalities and life directions?

Discontinuity

What happens when a family becomes separated, estranged, extinct, geographically dispersed or consciously rejects its earlier patterns?

Historical comparison

How can patterns be compared responsibly across periods without reducing complex societies to simple descriptions of class, morality or progress?

Evidence and falsifiability

What evidence would support the theory, what evidence would contradict it, and how could competing explanations be distinguished?

Proposed next stage

Develop precise terminology, review related academic literature, identify competing explanations, construct ethical research questions and define evidence capable of supporting or falsifying the theory. Any research involving people should use informed consent, appropriate privacy safeguards and independent ethical review where required.
